## A. Summary and Overview

Two things need saying before any result in this report.

The first is the review period. FCDO's award letter sets the first Annual Review at 15 October 2024 to 14 October 2025. Every figure available for this review, indicator and financial alike, was prepared on a 1 October 2024 to 30 September 2025 basis — that's the cycle partner reports came in on, and finance hasn't been recut. So every number here describes a twelve-month window that opens and closes a fortnight early. No written amendment to the period exists. The offset is unlikely to change the direction of any finding, but nothing in this report is a precise statement of contractual-period performance, and it shouldn't be read as one.

The second is the outcome. None of the programme's three outcome indicators has an achieved value for the year. The Annual Review is required to assess the Outcome. On the evidence held, that assessment can't be made.

The Girls Return to Learning and Safety Programme, FCDO programme code MWI-EDU-AR-4471, runs in Machinga and Mangochi districts, Malawi. FCDO approved £1,240,000 for the full period, 15 October 2024 to 14 October 2026, with inception to 31 December 2024. BridgeLight Education Trust delivers it alongside district education offices, community-based organisations, parent-teacher associations and girls' clubs.

Output delivery in year one was mixed. Ten of the twelve output indicators carry an achieved value. Five met or beat their Year 1 milestone: 684 girls re-enrolled or newly retained against a milestone of 650; 438 girls completing at least twenty remedial learning sessions against 420; 31 lockable latrine stances rehabilitated or newly functional against 24; 571 girls receiving a re-entry kit or learning materials package against 550; and 136 school and community actors trained against 120. Five fell short: 472 supported girls attending at least 80 per cent of school days against 500; 17 schools with menstrual health supplies and a trained focal teacher against 20; 392 caregivers receiving a hardship grant against 400; 68 per cent of hardship grant households with verified attendance follow-up inside 45 days against 75 per cent; and three district learning meetings against four.

Two indicators weren't reported at all — schools with an active safeguarding referral pathway tested through a termly case-review meeting, and learning briefs produced and shared with district partners. Both carried Year 1 milestones. That's a reporting gap rather than evidence of non-delivery, and it can't be resolved from the material available for this review.

Expenditure attributed to indicators came to £920,420 against a forecast of £880,000 — an overspend of £40,420, or 4.6 per cent. WASH rehabilitation drove most of it, with cement and transport running £27,900 above forecast. Output 1 field costs followed, pushed up by transport to lake-shore schools. Three lines underspent, the largest being attendance follow-up, where an M&E assistant post stood vacant. The programme-level forecast-against-actual statement the award letter requires hasn't been prepared. And the source data itself admits that cost attribution at indicator level is imperfect, with staff time recorded against attendance monitoring that may belong under Output 4.

What needs FCDO's attention comes down to five things. The outcome assessment can't be completed on the evidence provided. Two output indicators are unreported. The reporting period doesn't match the contract and hasn't been formally varied. Disaggregated figures don't reconcile to headline totals in three places, and the caregiver breakdown isn't credible on its face. And the proposed output scores in the export are marked draft and were prepared at indicator level rather than output level, so they aren't in a form FCDO can adopt at this review.

Four priorities follow, set out in full in the final section. BridgeLight should resolve the reporting period — either recut the data, or secure FCDO's written agreement to the October to September cycle. It should report the two missing output indicators. It should get outcome-level measurement running and report against it before the second Annual Review. And it should reconcile disaggregated data to headline figures before submitting any further results.

## B. Performance and Conclusions

**Outcome performance.** No achieved value exists for any of the three outcome indicators. That is the central finding of this review, and everything else in this section sits underneath it.

The outcome is that adolescent girls targeted by the programme show improved school retention, attendance and learning continuity by September 2026, with fewer practical and safeguarding barriers to education. Three indicators measure it: the share of supported girls attending school at least 80 per cent of days in the last completed term, baseline 38 per cent against a Year 1 milestone of 55; the share progressing to the next grade or completing a re-entry pathway, baseline 31 against a milestone of 48; and the share reporting school is safe during menstruation and travel, baseline 41 against a milestone of 58.

The results export prepared for this review holds output-level rows only. BridgeLight's monitoring design provides for quarterly survey tools, which would be the natural source, but no survey results have been provided. **The Annual Review therefore can't assess Outcome achievement for year one.** We're saying that plainly rather than working around it. Output data measuring related concepts does exist. But it counts individuals reached rather than the proportion of a defined population, and substituting it would produce a figure that looks like an outcome result and isn't one.

**Output performance against expected results.** Output 1, girls re-entering and remaining in education, weighted at 35 per cent, beat expectation on two indicators of three. Re-enrolment and retention reached 684 against a milestone of 650, drawn from school registers, re-entry club forms and a district validation sample. Remedial learning completion reached 438 against 420. Sustained attendance was the shortfall at 472 against 500, attributed to late register submission from four of the forty schools in scope.

Output 2, safer WASH and safeguarding conditions, weighted at 25 per cent, was mixed. Latrine rehabilitation reached 31 stances against a milestone of 24 — though four units still lack disposal bins, and the indicator doesn't make clear whether the figure counts stances made functional this year or includes the six already functional at baseline. Menstrual health provision fell short at 17 schools against 20, because three schools had no female focal teacher available. The third indicator, safeguarding referral pathways tested through case-review meetings, is unreported.

Output 3, reducing household cost barriers, weighted at 25 per cent and carrying the highest design risk rating, fell short on two indicators of three. Hardship grants reached 392 caregivers against a milestone of 400, after sixteen duplicate caregiver records were removed. Re-entry kits beat expectation at 571 against 550. Verified attendance follow-up inside 45 days reached 68 per cent against a milestone of 75, because September follow-ups were incomplete when reporting closed.

Output 4, district evidence use, weighted at 15 per cent, delivered three of four planned learning meetings — one was cancelled during the district examination period — and trained 136 school and community actors against a milestone of 120. Learning brief production is unreported.

**Major deviations.** Three deviations need FCDO's attention beyond individual indicator variance.

The absence of outcome data is the most consequential of the three, and it is dealt with above.

The unreported OP2.3 indicator matters out of proportion to its size. It's the programme's only measure of whether safeguarding systems work in practice rather than on paper, in a programme FCDO rated Medium-High risk partly for safeguarding sensitivity. Its absence isn't a missing number. It's a missing answer to the question the risk rating raises.

And the reported re-enrolment figure moved from 612 in a September internal note to 684 in the October export, described as the result of removing double-count risk. Deduplication reduces a count. It doesn't raise one by 72. The 684 is reported here as the verified figure because it comes from the later validated export, but the movement is unexplained and BridgeLight should explain it before FCDO adopts the number.

**Disaggregation.** The award letter requires monitoring data disaggregated by gender, age and vulnerability group where relevant, and the results export carries columns for all three. The data isn't currently reliable enough to meet that requirement.

For the two largest Output 1 indicators, the sex and age cells fall three and one short of the reported headline. For hardship grants, all 392 recipients are recorded as male and spread across age bands of 6–11, 12–17 and 18–24 — which cannot describe caregivers in a programme delivered substantially through mother groups. For district learning meetings, three meetings are disaggregated across person-level bands totalling 39, apparently describing attendees rather than the indicator itself. The vulnerability totals don't reconcile either: the previously married or early union column sums to 227 against a stated total of 291.

Where disaggregation does hold, it shows the pattern you'd expect. Girls aged 12–17 make up the large majority of those re-enrolled, attending regularly, completing remedial sessions and receiving kits, with smaller numbers in the 6–11 and 18–24 bands. Ultra-poor households and girls with disabilities appear across all four Output 1 and Output 3 indicators. One qualification: the monitoring age bands don't align with the stated target population of girls aged 10–19, so a share of those counted sit outside the intended range at both ends.

One further clarification, because the figure invites misreading. The totals row of the results export sums each column across all indicators. Those totals aren't counts of girls reached — the same girl appears under several indicators — and they must not be read as programme reach.

## Evidence and Evaluation

**Strength of the evidence base.** The evidence behind the output claims is administrative and, for the most part, fits what's being measured.

Re-enrolment rests on school registers and re-entry club forms with a district validation sample — the strongest evidence chain in the review. Attendance draws on term 3 registers from 36 of the 40 schools in scope. WASH claims are backed by engineer completion certificates and school verification photographs. Hardship grant delivery is evidenced by mobile money payment records with an attendance follow-up sample. Training and meeting claims rest on attendance sheets, with pre- and post-training quiz forms for the actor training indicator.

The pattern across these sources is consistent. BridgeLight can substantiate that activities happened and that girls and caregivers were reached. The award letter requires evidence for every numeric claim in the review, and for the ten reported output indicators that requirement is met in principle — though none of the underlying records has been examined as part of this review.

**Where the evidence base is weak.** It's weak in exactly the place that matters most.

There is no outcome-level evidence at all. The monitoring design names quarterly survey tools, which would generate the attendance, progression and safety-perception data the outcome indicators need. No survey output has been provided, and the documents don't say whether surveys were carried out.

Two output indicators have no evidence because they have no reported value. The safeguarding referral pathway indicator is the more consequential: it's the only measure in the framework that would show whether safeguarding systems work in practice.

**New evidence generated during the period.** None has been provided. Learning briefs would have been the programme's main evidence output, and their production is the unreported OP4.2 indicator — so this material can't say whether any were produced. Three of four district learning meetings were held with documented action points, but the minutes haven't been shared.

**Evaluation progress.** No evaluation activity is reported for the period and none appears to have been commissioned. That isn't a departure from plan; the programme design doesn't provide for an external evaluation in year one. But it does mean the review depends entirely on routine monitoring data.

**Data quality limitations.** Four limitations bear on how the results here should be read.

The reporting period offset affects every figure. All data is cut to 1 October 2024 to 30 September 2025 against a contractual period of 15 October 2024 to 14 October 2025.

Disaggregated data doesn't reconcile to headline figures in three indicators, and the caregiver breakdown isn't credible on inspection.

The re-enrolment figure moved upward by 72 during a process described as removing duplicates, and the movement is unexplained.

Cost attribution at indicator level is acknowledged in the source data to be imprecise, with staff time recorded under one output that may belong under another.

None of this undermines the broad picture of output delivery. Taken together, though, they mean individual figures should be treated as indicative rather than audited — and that the disaggregation requirement in the award letter isn't currently being met to a standard FCDO could rely on.

## Risk, Assumptions and Safeguarding

**Current risk position.** This review can't give one.

FCDO set an initial programme risk rating of Medium-High at award, reflecting delivery complexity across two districts, safeguarding sensitivity, construction-related procurement and household grant delivery. The programme design assigned output-level ratings: Medium for girls' re-entry and retention, Medium for WASH and safeguarding conditions, High for household cost barriers, Low for district evidence use. No updated ratings have been provided for the review period, and no risk register with mitigations, owners and current status exists in the material held.

So the table below reports the position at design and leaves the current column unfilled rather than inferring movement. Closing that gap is a recommendation of this review.

**Risks that materialised during the year.** Several design-stage risks are visible in the year's delivery data even without a formal register.

Construction cost inflation materialised. WASH rehabilitation overspent by £27,900 against a forecast of £121,000, on cement and transport, and it's the largest single financial variance in the programme.

School calendar disruption materialised. One of four district learning meetings was cancelled during the examination period, and four schools submitted attendance registers too late to count toward the reported figure.

Delivery capacity constraints materialised in a form the design didn't anticipate. Three schools couldn't be brought into the menstrual health indicator because no female focal teacher was available. And an M&E assistant post stood vacant, which both reduced spend on attendance follow-up and contributed to that indicator falling short of milestone.

**Assumptions.** The programme design doesn't set out an explicit assumptions register, so this review can't report on assumptions holding or failing in a structured way. Two implicit assumptions are visible in the data. The assumption that partner schools would report on a common cycle didn't hold, and it affects both the attendance indicator and the reporting period question. The assumption that mobile money coverage would support grant delivery appears to have held, with 392 caregivers paid — though payment fees ran above forecast.

**Risk of funds not being used as intended.** No incident is reported. Two observations are relevant. Sixteen duplicate caregiver records were identified and removed during the year, which shows payment verification is operating. Against that, verified attendance follow-up inside 45 days reached only 68 per cent against a milestone of 75, meaning roughly a third of hardship grant households hadn't been verified within the intended window when reporting closed. Follow-up verification is the main control linking payment to attendance, and its shortfall is the clearest fiduciary-adjacent weakness in the year.

**Climate and environment risk.** No climate or environmental risk assessment has been provided for the period, and the programme design doesn't contain one. The design identifies seasonal migration around Lake Malawi as a factor affecting the girls the programme works with, and lake-shore school locations are cited as the reason Output 1 transport costs exceeded forecast. Neither is presented as a climate risk in the source material, and this review doesn't treat them as one.

**Safeguarding.** No safeguarding update has been provided for the review period.

The programme has safeguarding procedures in place by design — child safeguarding procedures, a staff code of conduct, community complaints mechanisms, and referral pathways through district social welfare offices. FCDO identified safeguarding sensitivity as a driver of the Medium-High risk rating. And the one indicator that would evidence whether these systems function in practice, OP2.3, is unreported.

The absence of a safeguarding update must not be read as a nil return. It's an unanswered question, and it's one FCDO is entitled to have answered.

## F. Programme Management: Delivery, Commercial and Financial Performance

**Financial delivery.** Expenditure attributed to indicators totalled £920,420 against a forecast of £880,000 — an overspend of £40,420, or 4.6 per cent above plan. Six of ten cost lines overspent; four underspent.

The largest overspend is WASH rehabilitation at £148,900 against £121,000, driven by cement and transport. Output 1 field delivery follows at £174,850 against £162,000, on transport to lake-shore schools. Hardship grant delivery ran £5,440 over on payment fees, and learning materials £6,250 over after extra notebooks were bought locally. Actor training exceeded forecast by £4,630 on facilitator travel.

The underspends are smaller and mostly benign. Remedial teaching came in £6,670 under on lower teacher stipends. District meeting costs came in £6,300 under on lower venue costs, consistent with one meeting not taking place. Attendance follow-up underspent by £6,020 because an M&E assistant post was vacant — and that one carries a performance cost, because the same indicator fell short of its milestone. An underspend that buys a shortfall isn't a saving.

**Forecast against actual, and what's missing.** The figures above are indicator-attributed costs for the review year. They aren't a programme-level expenditure statement, and the award letter requires one covering forecast against actual for the approved award.

Two limitations matter here. The forecast column is explicitly stated in the source data to cover the review year only, not the life of the programme, so no life-of-programme burn position can be derived from it. And the source data acknowledges that attribution between indicators is imperfect, with staff time recorded against attendance monitoring that may belong under Output 4. A programme-level statement against the approved £1,240,000 is outstanding.

**Value for money.** The programme's business case set a cost of roughly £987 per directly supported girl over two years, calculated on the proposal budget of £1,184,000 and a target of 1,200 girls. That budget was superseded at award — FCDO approved £1,240,000 inclusive of an agreed contingency — so the business-case unit cost no longer reflects the approved envelope and should be restated at the next review.

On economy, the design commits to local procurement for rehabilitation works and competitive pricing for learning kits. Local purchasing is visible in the year's costs, though in the case of the additional notebooks it raised rather than reduced spend against forecast. Construction cost inflation, identified as a design risk, materialised and is the programme's largest variance.

On efficiency, the design proposes cost per girl re-enrolled, cost per school with completed WASH works, and staff cost as a share of total spend. None has been calculated for this review. And the imprecision in indicator-level cost attribution would limit how far the first two could be relied on even if they were.

On effectiveness, the intended measures are attendance, re-entry and progression. Re-entry performance beat its milestone. Attendance fell short. Progression can't be assessed, because outcome data is absent.

On equity, the intended measure is the share of support reaching girls with disabilities, girls from ultra-poor households and previously out-of-school girls. The results export carries these columns and they reconcile at column level. But the totals aggregate across overlapping indicators, so they can't be converted into a share of the individual girls and households supported. An equity assessment isn't possible on the current data structure.

**Partner and supplier performance.** The programme budgets £214,000 for local partner delivery over two years. No partner is named in the material provided, and no assessment of partner performance is available. Supplier performance is similarly unreported — though the WASH works generated verified completion certificates and school-level photographic verification, which suggests the technical supervision function is working. The four latrine units still awaiting disposal bins are the only specific delivery shortfall identified.

**Commercial and procurement issues.** Two are visible in the cost data. Construction materials and associated transport exceeded budget by a material margin, in a programme where construction inflation was flagged as a design risk. Menstrual health supplies were procured late, which contributed to that indicator falling three schools short of milestone. Neither appears to have been escalated in the material provided.

## Recommendations and Action Points

**Recommendations from this review.** Four actions are needed before the second Annual Review. Two of them decide whether that review can be completed at all.

The first is the reporting period. All AR1 data was prepared on an October to September basis against a contractual period of 15 October to 14 October. BridgeLight should either recut results and finance data to the contractual period, or seek FCDO's written agreement to the October to September cycle, as the award letter provides for. Carrying the ambiguity into a second year would compound it.

The second is outcome measurement. The programme has three outcome indicators with baselines and milestones, and nothing currently producing results against them. The monitoring design provides for quarterly surveys. Those need to be running — with a defined sample and a defined denominator — in time to report at AR2. Without that, the second Annual Review will be as unable to assess the Outcome as this one.

The third is completing the results framework. Two output indicators weren't reported. Both should be reported for the period now, and the results export should carry all twelve indicators as standard.

The fourth is data quality. Disaggregated figures should reconcile to headline totals before submission. The caregiver breakdown should be corrected to reflect adult recipients. Indicators that aren't person-level shouldn't carry person-level disaggregation. And the results export should distinguish clearly between column totals and counts of individual girls reached, so aggregate figures can't be misread as programme reach.

Beyond those four, three further points need action: a current risk register with ratings, mitigations and owners; a safeguarding update for the period, including a nil return if that's the position; and a partner performance assessment covering the £214,000 local delivery budget.

**Updates on previous recommendations.** Not applicable. This is the first Annual Review of the programme, so no previous review recommendations exist.

**Priorities for the next period.** Delivery priorities follow the pattern in the year's results. Sustained attendance, verified follow-up and menstrual health provision all fell short of milestone, and each carries a Year 2 target roughly double the Year 1 milestone — so recovery matters. The vacant M&E assistant post sits behind two of those shortfalls, and filling it should be treated as a delivery action rather than an administrative one. On the evidence side, the programme's own learning function, meetings and briefs, underperformed. That function is the mechanism through which Output 4 delivers its value.

## Appendix — what changed in the V4 pass, and why it is the reference register

Recorded so the ngo-reviewer judge's rubric can be derived from observed edits rather than restated rules.

**Structure.** Section A now opens with the two constraints that qualify everything beneath them — the period offset and the missing outcome — instead of programme identity. V4's structural rule is to start at the pressure point, and for a funder reading an Annual Review the pressure point is what the report cannot tell them.

**Contractions and register.** "Cannot" became "can't", "does not" became "doesn't", throughout the prose. Formality was reduced without loss of precision. Tables keep their terse register.

**Rhythm.** Long uniform sentences were broken. Short declaratives now carry the findings that matter: "This review can't give one." "Deduplication reduces a count." "An underspend that buys a shortfall isn't a saving." Sentences opening with "And" or "But" were introduced where the thought genuinely turns.

**Removed constructions.** "It should be noted that" (banned class). "Significant" used as an intensifier. Forced transitions between paragraphs where the subject simply changes.

**Sector vocabulary.** "Unique beneficiaries" became "the individual girls and households supported". "Beneficiary counts" became "counts of girls reached". "District stakeholders" became "district partners". V4's rule is to name who; in a donor report, naming who is also more accurate.

**Position and demand.** Findings that were stated neutrally now carry the judgment and the ask: the 684 movement should be explained "before FCDO adopts the number"; the OP2.3 absence is "a missing answer to the question the risk rating raises"; the safeguarding gap "must not be read as a nil return".

**Asymmetry.** The outcome finding gets more space and a sharper opening than the material around it, because it's the most consequential. Balanced airtime across findings of unequal weight is one of the five AI tells V4 exists to eliminate.

**What did not change.** Every number, date, name, claim map and table value. This was a prose pass. Any factual divergence from v1.0 is a defect, not an edit.
